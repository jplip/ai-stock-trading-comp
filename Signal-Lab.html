UNDERSTAND WHAT YOU BUILT

Make the explanation
as strong as the demo.

The presentation counts for 50% of the competition score. Use this playground to understand models and testing; present the actual competition notebook, submitted model, and its measured results. The browser experiment below tests its own next-open-to-open hypothesis.

01

Turn prices into clues

Historical price and volume behavior becomes numerical inputs, called features. Each feature uses information available by the signal day's close.

The target is whether the next open-to-open return will be positive. Predicting a direction is different from predicting an exact dollar price.

02

Ask three different models

Logistic regression learns a weighted combination of clues. A bootstrap forest combines decision trees that learn different rules. Nearest neighbors looks for similar historical situations.

The ensemble averages their uncalibrated up-scores, giving more weight to models with lower Brier error on validation data. These scores are not verified real-world success rates.

03

Turn a forecast into a position

If the model up-score passes your buy threshold, the strategy can hold stock. Otherwise, it holds cash. Volatility targeting can reduce stock exposure, conviction scales it, and your maximum exposure caps it.

Fees are charged when stock exposure changes. A confident prediction can still be wrong.

04

Keep the final exam honest

The first portion of history trains the models; the next selects ensemble weights. The last portion measures performance. Updates during the test can use newly known past outcomes, never future answers.

Repeatedly changing settings after seeing the test result turns that period into development data. A new, untouched test is then needed.

WHAT TO EXPLAIN TO THE JUDGES · 50% OF THE SCORE

The idea, the test, and what the result actually means.

For the submitted model, explain how the previous day's Open, High, Low, Close, and Volume become a scalar action from −1 to 1. Describe training on 2015–2022, then the 2023 practice evaluation. Explain the actual model and its decision rule, show its measured comparison with a baseline, and identify a limitation or failure case. Use the separate competition notebook for that evidence.

The three-model browser experiment has a different interface, feature history, allocation rule, and time split. Its screenshots and synthetic returns are learning aids, not submission results.

The numbers, in plain English

Return
The percentage change in simulated account value, after modeled fees.
Drawdown
How far the account fell from its previous highest value.
Sharpe ratio
Average bar return divided by its volatility, scaled using 252 bars per year. Cash return is assumed zero.
Direction accuracy
How often the predicted up/down direction matched the outcome. It does not measure profit.
Brier error
Average squared error of a probability forecast. Lower is better.
Basis point
One hundredth of a percentage point. A 10 bps charge is 0.10% per transaction side.

What this experiment leaves out

One stock, long or cash. No leverage, shorting, live feeds, or broker connection. It does not model taxes, dividends, market impact, slippage, or an order book.

Historical prices can contain gaps, corporate-action adjustments, and survivorship bias. Use consistently split-adjusted OHLC data. Daily bars are required, with 252 bars per year assumed for annualization. Intraday timestamps normalize to a date, and duplicate dates are rejected.

Small datasets, changing market conditions, and luck can all make a strategy look stronger than it is. A short competition score is limited evidence.

A simple format, ready to use.

Use a header row with Date, Open, High, Low, Close, Volume. Date, Open, and Close are required. Missing High/Low values are approximated from Open/Close; missing Volume becomes zero.
